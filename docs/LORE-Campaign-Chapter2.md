# Echoes of Legend
## Chapter Two: The Hundred-Year Truce

*Campaign narrative draft — written to be delivered in scenes, rival
encounters, battle barks, codex entries, and post-battle memories. It is
prose first: the mechanics are the shape beneath the story, not the story
itself.*

---

## 0. How this relates to Chapter One

**Same Wayfarer. New story. No prerequisites.**

The player is still the Wayfarer, still an Echo, still walking through the
afterlife collecting legends. That identity carries over and is the reason
this is Chapter Two and not a different game.

What does **not** carry over is the plot. No Road, no ten gates, no
Recruiter, no Quiet, no Gilgamesh, no Uruk. Chapter One was a closed story
and it stays closed: it ended with the Wayfarer's line in the ledger filled,
and this chapter does not reopen it, continue it, or depend on you having
played it.

The one thing the two share is a fact about the character, stated in one
line at the top of Chapter Two and never leaned on again:

> The Wayfarer walks out of one story and into the next with no name of their
> own, and a habit of collecting people who have one.

If you played Chapter One you know how that happened. If you did not, it is
simply who this person is. Neither reading is privileged.

**Deliberately a different shape:**

| | Chapter One | Chapter Two |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | a pilgrimage — ten gates, forward, alone | a **tournament** — one city, one week |
| Question | does my story deserve to last? | **who owns a story once it lasts?** |
| Tone | elegiac, quiet, sad | loud, political, funny, then not |
| Antagonist | grief with teeth | **an institution, and the good people in it** |
| The Wayfarer is | a blank being tested | a **nobody who files paperwork** |

---

## 1. The premise

Every hundred years the seven great houses of the afterlife hold **the
Concord** — a tournament to settle the only thing the dead can still
genuinely disagree about: *whose version is true*.

Stories drift. The living retell them and every retelling edits. A century
ago the houses agreed to stop settling versions in the street and start
settling them in an arena. One week, seven houses, one ruling. The winner
holds **the Canon** for a hundred years: the authority to say how every
contested legend is remembered.

It has worked. That is the problem.

**The Wayfarer arrives with a petition and no standing.**

They are nobody — no house, no name, no century of precedent. What they have
is twelve legends and a grievance that belongs to somebody else: a mercenary
captain called **Sargon**, no relation to the king, whose name a careless
scribe attached to another man's victories four hundred years ago.

Sargon is in the Canon as a hero. Sargon has spent four hundred years trying
to get it corrected, has petitioned eleven times, and has lost eleven times,
because the houses find the question embarrassing — nobody wants to open how
many *other* entries are wrong.

He is also dying. Not the ordinary way; Echoes do not do that. He is going
**thin** — the fate of a legend whose true story nobody carries any more.
Within a year there will be a hero in the Canon and nobody behind it.

He cannot file a twelfth petition. A petitioner must fight, and he can no
longer hold a sword.

So he asks the one person in the afterlife whose entire nature is carrying
other people's stories.

> "You've no name of your own. I've a name that isn't mine. Between us that's
> almost one honest man. Fight my twelfth."

---

## 2. What the houses want

The seven houses are the seven new factions. Each wants one thing written
into the Canon, each claim is defensible, and they cannot all be true.

| House | The claim it is fighting for |
|---|---|
| **Empyrean** | judgement is impartial — ratify our four hundred years |
| **Devaloka** | nothing is ever settled; the Canon should expire **yearly** |
| **Achaea** | mortals count; a legend needs no god's permission to be true |
| **Gehenna** | every version is for sale, and pretending otherwise is the real sin |
| **Jotunheim** | **abolish the Concord**; stories should be allowed to end |
| **Transylvania** | a story belongs to whoever **kept** it, not whoever lived it |
| **Tortuga** | the Canon is loot, and we are taking it |

The Wayfarer wants one line changed. One.

---

## 3. The week

Seven days, seven bouts, plus the two that are not on the schedule.

---

### XI — MEDEA, WHO IS PAID TO LOSE

**House:** Achaea · **Day one**

**Her situation.** Achaea cannot afford a real campaign, so they sell their
early bouts. Medea takes money from whichever house wants a soft opening and
loses convincingly. It is honest work and she is very good at it.

**Why she fights properly anyway.** She has read all eleven of Sargon's
petitions — she is the only person who has — and she thinks he is right.
"I've been paid to lose this. I have *not* been paid to insult you."

**Playstyle.** Achaea — Odysseus, Ajax, Atalanta, Medea. Nobody on her board
is finished when the fight starts; each becomes something better partway
through. **Bans your two most expensive cards.**

**Line.** "Everyone here is bought. The trick is knowing what for. I went for
eleven silver and a fair opinion of a dead mercenary, and I intend to deliver
both."

**Reward.** Odysseus + Atalanta. Coin. And the first ally.

---

### XII — THE DEBT-KEEPER, WHO RUNS THE BOOK

**House:** Gehenna · **Day two**

**His situation.** Gehenna's claim is that everything is for sale. He proves
it annually by buying results in the open and daring anyone to object —
objecting means conceding the Canon *can* be bought, which is his entire
argument.

**Why he fights you.** He offers to buy the twelfth petition. Not to bury it —
to **fund** it. He will hand over the Canon if the Wayfarer agrees to owe him,
and he will not say what for. "That is what a debt *is*. If you knew the
price you would call it a purchase."

Taking the deal is available, wins the chapter's easiest run, and is one of
its two bad endings.

**Playstyle.** Gehenna — Greed, Envy, Pride, Wrath. Every card visibly buys
its strength with something. **Bans your cheapest card** — he sets prices.

**Line.** "I have never lied to a bettor. I have simply noticed that nobody
reads the back of the tablet, and I stopped considering that my failing."

**Reward.** Greed + Envy. Coin.

---

### XIII — MICHAEL, WHO DOES NOT ASK

**House:** Empyrean · **Day three**

**His situation.** The Canon-holders do not fight their own early bouts. They
send Michael, who believes with total sincerity that Empyrean's four hundred
years have been fair, and who has never audited them, because it has not
occurred to him that he should.

**Why he fights you.** He was instructed to make the twelfth petition quick.
He was given no reason. He did not ask for one — and that incuriosity is the
whole character.

**The seam.** Mid-bout he refuses a ruling from his own officials that would
have won it for him. He offers no explanation, fights on, and loses.

**Playstyle.** Empyrean — Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Azrael. Everything is
announced a round before it lands; beaten by cancelling rather than
out-racing. **Bans your fastest card** — the court will not be hurried.

**Line.** "I am told the petitioner is a liar. I am also told not to ask who
told me. Only one of those instructions I intend to keep."

**Reward.** Azrael + Raphael. Coin. And a question that cannot be un-asked.

---

### XIV — CARMILLA, WHO KEEPS EVERYTHING

**House:** Transylvania · **Day four**

**Her situation.** Transylvania holds the archive — everyone's archive. That
is their claim: a story belongs to whoever *kept* it. She is genuinely the
reason half the roster still exists; houses that collapsed, legends nobody
has named in a thousand years, all cataloged and intact.

**Why she fights you.** The original record of Sargon's war is in her vault.
She will not lend it, sell it or copy it, because everything she has was
saved by refusing exactly those three requests from more sympathetic people
than a nameless Echo with a petition.

**Playstyle.** Transylvania — Dracula, Carmilla, Dorian Gray, Frankenstein's
Monster. What she strips off your board appears on hers. **Bans your
cleansers.**

**Line.** "Nine houses have burned since I started keeping. Every one of them
asked me to be reasonable first."

**Reward.** Carmilla + Dorian Gray. Coin. And the record.

---

### XV — THE TURN: WHAT THE RECORD SAYS

**No bout. The chapter's hinge.**

The original account of Sargon's war is intact, unedited, four hundred years
old. The Wayfarer reads it to him, because he cannot hold it steady any more.

**He did it.**

Not the Canon's version — no speech, no banner, no last stand. But the
load-bearing fact is true: he held a bridge for two days with forty people
and eleven hundred civilians crossed a river behind him, and he died on the
second afternoon in a way that was neither noble nor quick.

The Canon did not invent his heroism. It **tidied it.** It removed the forty
names. It removed the vomiting, the sergeant he had to threaten at knifepoint,
and the fact that he held the bridge because he had miscalculated the retreat
and was too proud to say so out loud.

Four hundred years insisting he was nobody — and the truth is he was somebody
he cannot stand.

**And the eleven petitions look different now.** He was never correcting the
record. He was **refusing a compliment**, for four centuries, because
accepting it meant accepting the forty men his pride got killed.

He tells the Wayfarer to withdraw the petition.

The Wayfarer does not.

---

### XVI — THE HERALD, WHO WANTS IT BURNED

**House:** Jotunheim · **Day five**

**His situation.** Jotunheim's claim: stories should be allowed to *end*. The
Canon props legends up past their natural death, endlessly retold, endlessly
edited, never permitted to finish. He finds the institution obscene.

**Why he fights you.** He has read the record too. He thinks the honest answer
is a bonfire, not a correction, and he will not back a petitioner who wants
one line fixed in a book that should not exist.

**Playstyle.** Jotunheim — Odin, Fenrir, Hel, Heimdall. Weak early, strongest
once legends start falling — the only deck that improves as the board empties.
**Bans your healers.**

**Line.** "Every one of his forty is still stood on that bridge because he
will not let them off it. Ask yourself who the Canon is serving now."

**Reward.** Odin + Fenrir. Coin.

---

### XVII — THE TURNER, WHO WANTS IT REWRITTEN YEARLY

**House:** Devaloka · **Day six**

**Her situation.** Annual revision is obviously fairer and completely
unworkable — six houses would campaign every year and none would ever govern.
She has proposed it at eleven Concords. It has never reached a vote.

**Why she fights you.** She will trade: her house's support for the petition,
in exchange for the Wayfarer's support for annual revision — a policy that
would wreck the thing the petition is trying to fix. The cleanest bad bargain
in the chapter.

**Playstyle.** Devaloka — Shiva, Kali, Ganesha, Durga. Setup and payoff as two
distinct jobs. **Bans your finishers** — you must build the kill, not find it.

**Line.** "You want one line changed. I want the whole book rewritten every
spring. We are the same complaint at different volumes, and only one of us
has been polite about it."

**Reward.** Ganesha + Kali. Coin.

---

### XVIII — THE QUARTERMASTER, WHO TAKES IT

**House:** Tortuga · **Day seven, at night**

**Their situation.** Tortuga's claim was always the honest one: the Canon is
loot, and the only reason nobody has taken it is that everyone agreed not to.
They have stopped agreeing. On the last night before the final, they steal it.

**Why she fights you.** Not over the theft — she offers the book. Genuinely,
no trick. A stolen Canon is worthless to a house nobody will trade with, and
she has worked out that its value was never the object. She wants the
Wayfarer to *use* it before the houses vote.

Which would win the correction by theft, before any ruling, in front of
everyone. The second bad ending, and very tempting.

**Playstyle.** Tortuga — Blackbeard, Anne Bonny, The Kraken, Davy Jones. Theft
as tempo: your advantages change sides rather than vanishing. **Bans your buff
support.**

**Line.** "Everyone in this city says *the Canon* like it's a mountain. It
weighs four pounds. I've carried heavier by accident."

**Reward.** Anne Bonny + Davy Jones. Coin. And a decision.

---

### XIX — THE NINTH LEDGER-KEEPER, WHO AUDITS

**House:** unaffiliated · **The morning of the final**

**His situation.** When Huaxia's house dissolved it left one official behind:
a clerk with the standing to audit the Canon's provenance, and no petitioner
to ask. He has kept the office and the hours. Four thousand mornings in a
room nobody enters.

**Why he fights you.** Because somebody finally files. And because a petition
granted without proof of competence gets overturned in a century, so he tests
the petitioner himself before he opens the book. "I will not have my first
audit in four hundred years thrown out on procedure."

**What the audit finds.** Empyrean did not forge the entry. A scribe did,
carelessly, four hundred years ago — exactly as Sargon always said. But
Empyrean **found the error two centuries ago and buried it**, because
admitting the Canon contains errors would collapse their claim to hold it.

Michael's refusal on day three makes sense now. He was never told why. He
declined to win anyway.

**Playstyle.** Huaxia, finally obtainable — Sun Wukong, Guan Yu, Zhuge Liang,
Qin Shi Huang. The counterplay deck: answers rather than initiates, and
punishes every habit the previous seven bouts built. **Bans nothing.** "Bring
your best. I have been bored for four thousand years."

**Line.** "Four thousand years is not devotion. Devotion gets tired. Four
thousand years is procedure."

**Reward.** Guan Yu + Zhuge Liang. Coin. And standing.

---

## 4. XX — THE FINAL: GABRIEL, WHO READS THE VERDICT

**House:** Empyrean · **The Concord floor, all seven houses watching**

Empyrean sends Gabriel, who has read every ruling for four hundred years. If
anyone can make a cover-up sound like continuity, it is the voice that
announced them all.

**He is not a hypocrite**, which is what makes him hard. He knew about the
error for two hundred years, he suppressed it, and he will say why to your
face: an institution that admits fallibility stops being obeyed, an
unobeyed Canon means seven houses settling versions in the street again, and
he has *seen* what that was like.

> "You want one line. I am protecting a century of peace from one line. Tell
> me which of us is being selfish, and be honest about the answer."

**Playstyle.** A bespoke boss card, unbannable and pinned. His signature deals
no damage: it **overrules** — one of your legends is declared to have acted
differently, its last action undone and replayed as the *Canon version*, his
choice. A boss that edits your history mid-fight, which is the chapter's theme
as a mechanic. His twelve is Empyrean's court plus four hundred years of
legends whose entries were also quietly wrong. **Bans your two strongest
cards** — "the record will reflect that you were never permitted them."

---

## 5. The ending

The Wayfarer wins, and holds the Canon for a century, and has to use it in
front of every house. Three doors:

**1. Correct the line.** Sargon's forty are named. The Canon survives with
its authority intact — which means the suppression *worked*, the next
scribe's error gets buried too, and somebody in four hundred years files
their twelfth petition.

**2. Publish the audit.** Empyrean's credibility burns, the Canon is proven
fallible, the houses go back to settling versions in the street. The Herald
gets his bonfire. The forty stay unnamed, because nobody is keeping records
any more.

**3. Both, and then give it away.** Correct the line, publish the audit, and
**abolish the century** — rule that the Canon passes not every hundred years
but whenever a petition is *proven*, by audit, by anyone. No holder, no
tenure, no incumbent with four centuries to protect. It is the Turner's
reform at a workable interval, and the only thing that could ever have bought
it is a Canon-holder surrendering the Canon on his first morning.

The Wayfarer holds it for a day and a half — the shortest tenure in the
history of the Concord. The Ninth Ledger-Keeper files the paperwork and, for
the first time in four thousand years, has a successor to train.

**The cost.** The corrected entry is not the heroic one. It has the
miscalculation in it, and the threatened sergeant, and the vomiting, and the
forty names. Crowds do not cheer it. Within thirty years nobody performs
Sargon's story at all — it is not *performable*.

He goes thin anyway. Slower, and on his own terms, and while somebody is
reading the true version aloud to him.

The forty do not. Their names are in the book, forty separate entries, each
true and dull and permanent, and no scribe can tidy them because the audit
that made them is attached.

That was always the trade. He needed four hundred years to be able to say it
out loud, and somebody else's hands to file it.

## 6. Epilogue — The Next Blank Line

The Wayfarer leaves the city with twelve legends, a coin purse, and no more
claim to a name than they arrived with.

The Ninth Ledger-Keeper stops them at the gate and hands over a blank tablet,
which is either a formality or a joke, and with him it is impossible to tell.

"Petition form," he says. "For when you want your own line looked at."

"I don't have a line."

"No," he agrees. "That is generally the point at which people begin one."

Ahead, the road out is busy — houses striking their pavilions, a crowd
arguing about a ruling that will not be a ruling for very much longer, and
somewhere out past all of it, the next city.

Somewhere, in a world that had almost forgotten them, a page turns.

---

## 7. Why this works

- **The Wayfarer identity carries; the plot does not.** Same character, same
  premise, zero prerequisites. Play it first if you like.
- **The Wayfarer's namelessness is finally an asset instead of a wound** —
  it is precisely why Sargon picks them, and it is the last beat of the
  chapter.
- **The antagonist is an institution** with motives, officials who disagree
  with each other, and a defence that is genuinely good.
- **The turn at XV inverts the premise:** he was telling the truth, and the
  truth is worse than the lie he was fighting.
- **Two bad endings are reachable and tempting** (Gehenna's debt, Tortuga's
  theft), so the good one is a choice rather than a corridor.
- **The boss mechanic is the theme** — a card that edits your history
  mid-fight.
- **Huaxia unlocks at XIX**, as the auditor nobody called.
